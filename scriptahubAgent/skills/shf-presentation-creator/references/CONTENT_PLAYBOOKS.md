# Content-specific directing plans

These are working plans, not a universal mandatory sequence. Percentages allocate a first draft of a 5–10-minute film; voice measurement determines the actual duration. Short sources may warrant shorter films. Each scene still needs a source record, visual action and voice direction.

## Purpose precedes genre

For a book-page introduction or reading invitation, use [BOOK_INTRODUCTION.md](BOOK_INTRODUCTION.md)
as the controlling plan. The genre plans below supply techniques for selected
content samples; their walkthrough structure must not override the goal of
making readers understand why the book deserves their time.

## Shared dramatic contract

Open a question, make it matter, provide increasing understanding, acknowledge the hardest qualification, and deliver a satisfying synthesis. Alternate concrete examples with abstraction. A “shock” must follow from the source, not from sensational wording. Never invent danger, conflict, statistics or a research breakthrough to improve retention.

For each plan, make a hook/payoff ledger: `question`, `introducedSceneId`, `resolvedSceneId`, `resolution`, `sourceClaimIds`, `status`. A deliberately unanswered mystery must be explicitly labelled `open-by-design`, not forgotten.

## 1. Science fiction / speculative fiction

**Promise:** understand the world's unusual premise and the choice it creates
for its inhabitants, who need not be human. In a book introduction, use only
premise-level discoveries and world rules. The reversal and resolution beats
below describe a summary when authorized; never reveal them merely to fill the
structure of a reading invitation. Develop a source-specific art identity rather
than recycling a technical explainer's cast and layouts.

| Range | Narrative work | Visual / vocal direction |
|---|---|---|
| 0–8% | A concrete anomaly contradicts ordinary expectation. | One familiar object behaves unexpectedly; restrained wonder, not a trailer announcer. |
| 8–22% | Establish one person, desire, world rule and cost. | Consistent character + location; demonstrate the rule through action. |
| 22–42% | First consequential discovery. | Reveal a new relation or layer, anchored to the character's investigation. |
| 42–62% | The premise becomes a conflict of values. | Two plausible choices, not a good/bad color cliché. |
| 62–82% | A reversal changes the meaning of the discovery. | Reuse the opening motif in a changed context. |
| 82–95% | Articulate the central dilemma and what the story explores. | Slow the motion; let the person visibly consider alternatives. |
| 95–100% | Pay off the teaser promise; invite reading without spoiling beyond brief. | A final image and one specific question, not a generic call to action. |

**Example:** “A library can preserve a civilization. Could it also overrule one?” Contrast archival care, prediction and authority. Do not present all three as equivalent intelligence.

**Fail condition:** the viewer remembers a planet and a glowing object but cannot explain the dilemma. Repair by showing what the protagonist stands to lose.

## 2. Literary fiction / drama

**Promise:** understand an emotional or ethical tension, not every plot event.

0–10%: a small gesture with disproportionate meaning. 10–30%: person, relationship and unspoken desire. 30–50%: two or three events that make their tension visible. 50–70%: the strongest competing interpretation of the person's action. 70–90%: a changed understanding, with ambiguity preserved. 90–100%: synthesize the theme and return to the opening gesture.

**Stage:** a room, a table, a letter, a door; recurring spatial distance between people. Express emotion through gaze, hesitation, posture and the handling of objects. Avoid a large grin for every positive sentence or exaggerated tears for every sad one.

**Hook:** “She keeps the letter. She never sends it. What, then, is it for?” Use only when supported by the story; otherwise choose an actual action.

**Fail condition:** a diagnosis or moral judgement is asserted where the text allows several readings. Attribute interpretations and preserve uncertainty.

## 3. Mystery / suspense

**Promise:** show why the puzzle is worth following without accidentally revealing its answer.

0–8%: the impossible-looking observation. 8–25%: known facts and stakes. 25–45%: two explanations, each with a weakness. 45–65%: a clue changes which detail matters. 65–85%: distinguish evidence from suspicion. 85–100%: explain the *type* of puzzle and thematic stake, with a clearly declared spoiler cutoff.

Track `knownToViewer`, `knownToCharacter`, and `spoilerLevel` in the editorial plan. A withheld reveal must not leave the film's own explanation incoherent. Use anchored evidence objects; do not draw unsupported causal arrows between suspects and events.

## 4. Philosophy / conceptual argument

**Promise:** replace an apparently simple question with a more precise and useful distinction.

0–10%: a concrete paradox or choice. 10–25%: define the disputed terms. 25–45%: reconstruct the strongest version of the thesis. 45–60%: walk through a thought experiment. 60–78%: introduce a serious objection or rival view. 78–92%: show what survives the objection. 92–100%: answer the opening question with the correct qualifications.

**Stage:** doors for options; strings for influences only when explicitly explained; mirror for reflection; balance for competing reasons. Change one variable at a time. Do not make an attractive metaphor do the work of a logical inference.

**Voice:** reflective, lucid, unhurried around definitions; a skeptical line should sound like a genuine challenge, not ridicule.

**Hook:** “Doing what you want sounds like freedom. Who decided what you want?” Pay off by separating theories, not by claiming desires prove nobody is free.

## 5. Technical architecture / engineering explanation

**Promise:** understand the problem, a mechanism and the conditions under which it helps.

0–8%: a concrete failure with consequences. 8–20%: current workflow and constraints. 20–45%: follow one input through the proposed mechanism. 45–62%: show an intermediate state, inspection point or invariant. 62–78%: compare against a real alternative on named dimensions. 78–91%: demonstrate a failure case or boundary. 91–100%: summarize the actual contribution and what an engineer could test next.

**Stage:** document → operation → result, but every object must represent a real artifact. A claim card, source document and approval stamp have different meanings. A trace shows what happened; it does not itself prove the answer is true.

**Novelty:** use “the author proposes” when no independent literature check establishes novelty. A demo is not a benchmark; qualitative comparison is not a speedup chart.

**Hook:** “The answer is useful. Why can the organization still not use it?” The payoff can be provenance, authorization or intervention, but must follow the source.

## 6. Research paper / experimental result

**Promise:** know what was asked, what was measured, and what the result does and does not establish.

0–10%: the key empirical question or counterintuitive result, qualified. 10–25%: gap and hypothesis. 25–45%: setup, controls and measured outcome. 45–62%: main result with sourced scale/units. 62–78%: alternative explanations, limitations or negative results. 78–92%: implication supported by the evidence. 92–100%: open test that would change confidence.

Use a visible distinction between supplied rules, learned behavior, measurement and interpretation. Show sample size or benchmark identity when material. Do not animate a tiny toy result into a sweeping world-changing conclusion. If charts are not supported by source data, use clearly labelled conceptual illustrations instead.

## 7. History / social change

**Promise:** understand interacting causes and a consequential change, not memorize a timeline.

0–10%: an ordinary life before/after a change. 10–25%: geography, institutions and period. 25–45%: two or three causal pressures. 45–65%: event or decision that changes constraints. 65–82%: different consequences for different groups. 82–95%: rival interpretations or contested evidence. 95–100%: return to the opening life with a more informed explanation.

**Stage:** map, routes, city/institution, clock; a map zoom must preserve geographic meaning. Separate chronology from causation. Historical people's appearance and motives require evidence; a generic actor can be labelled representative rather than a portrait.

## 8. Biography / personal narrative

**Promise:** see how a person responds to constraints and choices, without pretending hindsight made the outcome inevitable.

0–10%: a decision or contradiction. 10–28%: relevant context and relationships. 28–50%: a turning point with plausible alternatives. 50–70%: cost, failure or revision. 70–90%: consequences and limits of personal agency. 90–100%: the distinctive lesson, not a universal motivational slogan.

Keep identity stable. Do not infer mental illness, private motives or moral character from a generic gesture. If voice generation concerns a real person, use a licensed narrator rather than cloning their voice without authorization.

## 9. Popular science / explanatory lesson

**Promise:** the viewer can explain or predict one mechanism after watching.

0–10%: observable phenomenon. 10–25%: ask the viewer to predict what happens when one condition changes. 25–50%: reveal the mechanism in three clear steps. 50–65%: apply it to a second concrete case. 65–82%: a common misconception and counterexample. 82–95%: a final prediction with explanation. 95–100%: compact synthesis.

The “lesson” is not a quiz gimmick. Make the prediction answerable from what the film teaches. Animate variables, not irrelevant lab decorations. For medical subjects, distinguish educational explanation from personal advice and follow the environment's accuracy requirements.

## 10. Practical tutorial / how-to

**Promise:** complete a bounded task and understand the most important failure mode.

0–10%: finished result and prerequisites. 10–25%: mental model. 25–60%: a small number of visible operations, each with before/after. 60–80%: intentional error and recovery. 80–93%: verify success rather than merely assuming it. 93–100%: recap and next bounded task.

Show real command names/inputs only when correct and legible. The SHF player is not a code execution sandbox; represent results as illustrations unless a separate trusted tool actually ran them. Keep detailed code in the transcript or project file, not a tiny moving paragraph.

## 11. Business / policy / organisational argument

**Promise:** clarify a decision, beneficiaries, costs and trade-offs without turning uncertain forecasts into promises.

0–10%: costly friction or a hard choice. 10–28%: actors and constraints. 28–48%: proposed intervention and mechanism. 48–68%: strongest alternative and comparison dimensions. 68–85%: who bears the risk, what could fail, what is unknown. 85–95%: decision conditions and measurable next experiment. 95–100%: conditional synthesis.

Avoid exaggerated financial/health outcomes. Use sourced numbers or no numbers. A human approval icon means a defined approval step, not a blanket assertion of legal compliance.

## 12. Mixed anthology / long technical report

**Promise:** reveal the organizing thread while preserving important disagreements between sections.

Start with a map of the central question, not a table of contents. Cluster sections by question or mechanism. Give each cluster a mini-arc: claim, example, caveat, relation to the whole. Use recurring visual locations for clusters so viewers can navigate. Finish by distinguishing agreement, tension and genuinely unresolved issues.

Do not average conflicting viewpoints into a synthetic consensus. Maintain source attribution at beat level. If a section does not support the chosen central thread, record its omission explicitly rather than inventing a connection.

## Scaling the plans

A 90-second teaser uses one concrete situation, one unexpected turn and one insight; it cannot honestly reconstruct an entire technical debate. A 5–10-minute visual essence uses the full selected plan. A 30–60-minute companion repeats chapter-level arcs without repeating the same opening trick. Maintain a topic ledger and visible chapters, place short synthesis moments between them, and reduce music under dense material. Validate long-form comfort through actual viewing, not only timeline length.
